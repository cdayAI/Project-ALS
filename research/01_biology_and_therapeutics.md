# The Current State of ALS Biology and Therapeutics
**A critical research brief (literature current through 2024–2025)**

*Sources verified via NCBI PubMed E-utilities; PMIDs cited inline. Items known only from company/regulatory announcements (no peer-reviewed record yet) are explicitly flagged.*

---

## 1. Core Disease Mechanisms — with evidence grading

ALS is best understood as a **syndrome of converging downstream pathways** triggered by diverse upstream causes (genetic, environmental, stochastic). No single mechanism explains all cases, but several are very well supported.

| Mechanism | Evidence strength | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| TDP-43 proteinopathy | **Very strong** | Present in ~97% of ALS cases at autopsy |
| C9orf72 repeat expansion + DPR toxicity | **Very strong** (as cause) / moderate (mechanistic detail) | Most common genetic cause; ~40% fALS, ~7% sALS |
| SOD1 misfolding | **Strong** (for SOD1-ALS subset) | ~2% of all ALS; toxic gain-of-function |
| RNA dysregulation | Strong | Shared theme across TDP-43/FUS/C9orf72 |
| Neuroinflammation (microglia/astrocytes) | Strong | Non-cell-autonomous death is established |
| Mitochondrial dysfunction & impaired axonal transport | Moderate–strong | Reproducible in models; causal role unclear |
| Glutamate excitotoxicity | Moderate | Basis of riluzole's effect; downstream event |
| Prion-like seeded spread | Moderate | Explains anatomical progression staging |

### 1.1 TDP-43 proteinopathy (~97% of cases)
Neumann et al. identified TDP-43 as the major disease protein in the ubiquitinated, tau-/α-synuclein-negative inclusions that define ALS and FTLD: pathologic TDP-43 is hyperphosphorylated, ubiquitinated, and cleaved to C-terminal fragments, recovered only from affected CNS regions (PMID 17023659). This pathology is present in essentially all sporadic ALS and most genetic forms — regardless of which gene initiated disease — making TDP-43 the single most conserved endpoint in ALS biology (PMID 40252666). Critically, mutations in TARDBP itself are rare (3–5% of familial, <1% of apparently sporadic cases), so TDP-43 aggregation is almost always a downstream convergent event, not a direct mutational cause (PMID 40252666). Mechanistically, loss of nuclear TDP-43 function (RNA splicing/stability) and gain of cytoplasmic aggregation are both implicated; disentangling which kills motor neurons remains unsolved (PMIDs 39337454, 37890889).

### 1.2 C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion
An intronic (GGGGCC)n expansion in C9orf72 is the most common genetic cause of ALS and FTD (PMID 37847372). Three non-mutually-exclusive toxic mechanisms are supported:
1. **Dipeptide repeat (DPR) proteins** produced by repeat-associated non-AUG translation — poly(GR)/poly(PR) are potently neurotoxic; suppressor screens confirm toxicity pathways in vivo (PMID 36931278).
2. **RNA foci** that sequester RNA-binding proteins and dysregulate splicing; G4C2 RNA co-localizes with and disrupts nuclear speckles, impairing splicing integrity (PMID 39181135).
3. **Haploinsufficiency** of C9orf72 protein (reduced function), though this appears insufficient alone.
The field now favors DPR toxicity plus partial loss-of-function acting together (PMID 37847372).

### 1.3 SOD1 misfolding
SOD1 mutations account for ~2% of all ALS (PMIDs 41661214, 39820998). Disease operates via a toxic gain of function from misfolded, aggregated SOD1 — the mechanistic foundation for knockdown therapy (tofersen), which validated the target clinically (Section 3). SOD1-ALS also provided the dominant animal model for two decades (with major caveats, Section 5).

### 1.4 FUS
Mutations in FUS cause ~0.3–0.9% of ALS, typically young-onset, rapidly progressive disease (PMID 39862884). Like TDP-43, FUS is an RNA-binding protein with a C-terminal nuclear localization signal; mutations impair autoregulation, splicing regulation, and nuclear localization, causing cytoplasmic accumulation (PMID 24204307). Knock-in mice expressing humanized mutant FUS reproduce key phenotypes, supporting both gain- and loss-of-function contributions (PMID 35075293).

### 1.5 RNA dysregulation
A unifying thread: TDP-43 and FUS bind thousands of RNAs; C9orf72 repeats sequester splicing factors and disrupt nuclear speckles (PMID 39181135); cryptic exons generated by TDP-43 loss create neoantigens. This positions ALS fundamentally as an RNA-processing disease for the large majority of cases.

### 1.6 Neuroinflammation (microglia/astrocytes)
Motor neuron death is substantially **non-cell-autonomous**: activated microglia and reactive astrocytes, plus infiltrating peripheral immune cells, actively contribute to degeneration (reviewed PMID 38775138). Genetic ALS mutations act cell-autonomously in glia as well as neurons (PMID 38775138). Clinical corroboration: PB/TURSO reduced plasma YKL-40 (~20%) and CRP (~30%) in CENTAUR (PMID 38050066), and masitinib's rationale is mast-cell/glial tyrosine-kinase inhibition (PMIDs 27400786, 34457038).

### 1.7 Mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired axonal transport
Bioenergetic failure, mitochondrial fragmentation, and disrupted axonal transport of mitochondria and organelles are reproducible across ALS models and patient tissue (reviewed PMIDs 39337454, 40252666, 37890889). CNM-Au8 (bioenergetic catalysis) was built on this hypothesis (PMID 33431491) — its clinical trial failure (Section 4) shows the pathway is real but not yet therapeutically tractable.

### 1.8 Glutamate excitotoxicity
Reduced EAAT2/GLT1 glutamate clearance in astrocytes elevates synaptic glutamate; excitotoxic stress contributes to motor neuron death (reviewed PMIDs 39337454, 37296644). Riluzole's anti-glutamatergic action producing a small but real survival benefit is the strongest clinical validation of any single pathway in ALS.

### 1.9 Prion-like seeded aggregation spread
pTDP-43 pathology spreads through the CNS in stereotyped stages — from agranular motor cortex, brainstem motor nuclei, and spinal cord α-motoneurons outward to other regions — consistent with cell-to-cell propagation of misfolded seeds (PMID 23686809). This supports a "molecular Braak" model of ALS progression and motivates spread-blocking strategies, though no anti-spread therapeutic has yet reached proof of concept in patients.

## 2. Approved Drugs — what they actually do (honest effect sizes)

| Drug | Mechanism | Approval | Real effect size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Riluzole 50 mg BID | Anti-glutamatergic | 1995 (FDA) | HR ~0.72 for tracheostomy-free survival; median life extension ≈ **2–3 months** |
| Edaravone IV | Free-radical scavenger | 2017 (FDA), selected population | −1.55 ALSFRS-R points over 24 weeks vs placebo; no proven mortality benefit |
| PB/TURSO (Relyvrio/Albrioza) | ER-stress + mitochondrial apoptosis inhibitor | Sept 2022 (FDA) | 0.42 pts/month slower ALSFRS-R decline; **withdrawn 2024 after Phase 3 failure** (below) |
| Tofersen (Qalsody) | ASO knockdown of SOD1 mRNA | Apr 2023 (FDA); conditional EMA Sept 2024 | Biomarker-dramatic, clinically modest; **~2% of ALS patients eligible** |

### 2.1 Riluzole
Cochrane meta-analysis (4 RCTs, 974 riluzole vs 503 placebo): pooled hazard ratio 0.72 for tracheostomy-free survival at 100 mg/day; benefit equivalent to roughly 2–3 months of median survival, modest functional effects, benign safety profile (PMID 22419278). Still the backbone of care nearly 30 years post-approval.

### 2.2 Edaravone
Approved on MCI186-ALS19: in a narrowly defined early-stage subpopulation (definite/probable El Escorial, FVC ≥80%, all ALSFRS-R items ≥2, 1–4 point decline during pre-randomization observation), edaravone slowed ALSFRS-R decline by 1.55 points over 24 weeks (PMID 28522181). No demonstrated survival benefit; real-world effectiveness data are mixed (PMID 35006266). Oral formulation approved 2022 for convenience.

### 2.3 Sodium phenylbutyrate/taurursodiol (PB/TURSO; Relyvrio in US, Albrioza in Canada)
CENTAUR (n=137): slowed ALSFRS-R decline 0.42 points/month (P=0.03); **no secondary endpoint significant**, including survival in the randomized phase (PMID 32877582). Open-label long-term follow-up suggested median OS 25.0 vs 18.5 months (HR 0.56, P=0.023; ~6.5-month median survival difference) (PMID 33063909).
- **Controversy:** FDA advisory committee initially voted the evidence inadequate (March 2022); approval followed a re-analysis and unprecedented advocacy pressure. Commentators documented "questionable regulatory actions," including reconvening the advisory committee (PMID 38646691).
- **Resolution — Phase 3 failure:** The confirmatory PHOENIX Phase 3 trial (n≈660) failed to meet its primary endpoint or key secondary endpoints (company announcement, October 2024 — *no PubMed publication yet*). Amylyx subsequently **voluntarily withdrew Relyvrio/Albrioza from the US market (effective early 2025) and Canada**. As of 2025 the drug is no longer marketed for ALS. The CENTAUR result now looks most consistent with a false-positive or at best very small effect — the cautionary tale of the era.

### 2.4 Tofersen (Qalsody) — SOD1-ALS only
VALOR Phase 3 (n=108, ~2% of ALS patients have pathogenic SOD1 variants): **primary endpoint missed** — ALSFRS-R change at week 28 was −6.98 (tofersen) vs −8.14 (placebo), difference 1.2 points with confidence interval crossing null. But biomarker effects were profound: CSF SOD1 cut roughly in half and plasma NfL reduced ~55%, indicating target engagement and reduced axonal injury (PMID 36129998). Integrated VALOR + open-label extension analysis through 148 weeks showed earlier initiation associated with less decline (ALSFRS-R −9.9 vs −13.5; SVC −13.8% vs −18.1%) and prolonged survival versus expected SOD1-ALS natural history (PMID 41661214). Meta-analysis confirms consistent ALSFRS-R benefit across studies (SMD 0.44, P=0.03) (PMID 39820998). Honest reading: tofersen slows a fast disease by months, works best early, and its approval rests heavily on biomarker + delayed-start logic rather than a positive primary endpoint (PMID 39330700). The ATLAS trial tested presymptomatic initiation in SOD1 carriers selected by NfL elevation; topline results (June 2024 conference report — *peer-reviewed publication pending*) showed NfL lowering without measurable delay of symptom onset within the trial window — a sobering signal that neuronal injury may begin before currently detectable biomarker thresholds (design: PMID 35585374).

## 3. Live Pipeline 2024–2025

### 3.1 Masitinib (oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor; mast cells/microglia)
Phase 2/3 AB10015 (n=394, add-on to riluzole) used a prospectively defined enrichment: in "normal progressors" (ΔFS <1.1 pts/month), masitinib 4.5 mg/kg/d slowed decline (time to 7-point ALSFRS-R loss; P=0.027 vs placebo) (PMID 31280619). Long-term survival analysis in the enriched cohort reported median OS 69 vs 44 months (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31–0.92) — an extraordinary claim requiring confirmation (PMID 34457038). Confirmatory Phase 3 (AB19001) has been running since ~2019; results awaited. Regulatory status unresolved in US/EU as of mid-2025. Hepatotoxicity is a recognized risk (PMID 36048877). If confirmed, this would be the largest survival effect ever shown in ALS — treat with calibrated skepticism until readout.

### 3.2 Reldesemtiv (fast skeletal muscle troponin activator)
FORTITUDE-ALS Phase 2b suggested dose-dependent slowing (PMID 36930042 context). The definitive COURAGE-ALS Phase 3 (n=486 randomized, hybrid decentralized design) **stopped for futility at the second interim analysis**: mean group difference in ALSFRS-R change at week 24 was −1.1 points (95% CI −2.17 to −0.08; P=0.04) — numerically *favoring placebo* — and the combined assessment also showed no benefit (win ratio 0.91, P=0.11) (PMID 40126464). Program effectively dead for ALS. A symptomatic-muscle approach that failed to translate.

### 3.3 CNM-Au8 (nanocrystalline gold; bioenergetic catalysis)
Tested in the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial (161 randomized within regimen): **missed the primary endpoint** (disease rate ratio not significantly <1) though some secondary and biomarker outcomes trended favorable (PMID 40067821). Sponsor continued the separate PHOENIX CSF-biomarker study into 2025, but there is no efficacy signal strong enough to support approval expectations. Note the platform-trial pattern: CNM-Au8 negative, verdiperstat negative (PMID 40067754) — HEALEY has produced many clean negatives efficiently, which is itself informative about the pipeline.

### 3.4 WVE-004 (stereopure ASO, C9orf72)
Variant-selective stereopure oligonucleotide designed to knock down repeat-containing transcripts while sparing wild-type C9orf72 — dose-dependently reduces mutant transcripts in patient-derived neurons (PMID 35592494). Completed Phase 1b/2 in C9orf72-ALS/FTD with biomarker data presented late 2024 (*conference/company report — no PubMed publication found*); subsequent development pace slowed as sponsor prioritization shifted. Target engagement appears achievable; clinical benefit unproven. Broader ASO lessons from tofersen apply: knockdown is feasible; timing may be everything (PMID 39473221).

### 3.5 ION363 (jacifusen) — FUS-ALS
Intrathecal ASO silencing FUS. Published case series reports molecular engagement and clinical outcomes in FUS-ALS patients treated compassionately/prospectively (PMID 40414240); FUS-ALS is ultra-rare (0.3–0.9% of ALS), so evidence will remain small-series based (disease context: PMID 39862884). Preclinical knockdown work validates the strategy (PMID 35075293).

### 3.6 Gene therapy approaches
Reviews map a crowded preclinical-to-clinical landscape: AAV-mediated knockdown/replacement, CRISPR-based editing (e.g., in-vivo base editing of SOD1 in mice, PMID 31991108), delivered intrathecally or systemically (PMIDs 33839324, 35805149). Field-wide roadmap established at the 2023 C9orf72 summit (PMID 37847372). No gene therapy has yet completed a successful pivotal ALS trial as of 2025.

### 3.7 Stem cells
- **NurOwn (autologous MSC-NTF):** Phase 3 failed its primary responder-analysis endpoint; intrathecal MSC Phase 3 programs did not meet primary efficacy endpoints (trial registration PMID 34890069; failure noted in PMID 37851042). FDA rejected resubmission.
- **Neuralstem (human spinal cord-derived neural stem cells):** Phase 1 microinjection trials established surgical safety and feasibility of intraspinal transplantation (PMIDs 24018694, 31104357) — a methodological achievement, but no efficacy pathway to approval has emerged.

### 3.8 Notable Phase 3 failures (the graveyard that defines the field)
| Agent | Trial | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Dexpramipexole | EMPOWER, n=943 (PMID 24067398) | Failed CAFS primary endpoint despite striking Phase 2 signal |
| Reldesemtiv | COURAGE-ALS (PMID 40126464) | Stopped for futility; trend favored placebo |
| PB/TURSO | PHOENIX (2024, company announcement) | Primary + key secondary missed; drug withdrawn from market |
| CNM-Au8 | HEALEY regimen (PMID 40067821) | Missed primary DRR endpoint |
| Verdiperstat | HEALEY regimen (PMID 40067754) | Negative |
| NurOwn MSC-NTF | Phase 3 (PMIDs 34890069, 37851042) | Missed primary responder endpoint |
| Edaravone (full population) | Earlier Phase 3 | Negative; approved only via enriched subpopulation re-analysis (PMID 28522181) |

## 4. The Honest Bottleneck Analysis — why no cure exists

**1. Heterogeneity.** ALS is many diseases sharing one phenotype: distinct genetic causes (C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, FUS, >50 genes total), divergent speeds, sites of onset, and likely different initiating mechanisms converging on common endpoints (PMIDs 40252666, 40283201). A drug targeting one upstream cause helps ≤2–10% of patients; a drug must hit a shared downstream node to be broad — but those nodes (TDP-43, neuroinflammation) have resisted pharmacology. Mixed populations dilute signals: riluzole, edaravone, PB/TURSO, and masitinib all show their clearest effects in enriched subpopulations.

**2. Diagnosis latency.** Motor neurons are dying silently before symptoms: up to ~50% of lower motor neurons may already be lost at clinical onset, and blood NfL rises months before phenoconversion in presymptomatic mutation carriers (PMID 35585374). By diagnosis, patients are being treated in the endgame. ATLAS's inability to delay onset even with presymptomatic treatment suggests either irreversible early injury or intervention still too late (June 2024 topline; design PMID 35585374).

**3. Biomarker poverty.** Until recently, ALS had **zero validated pharmacodynamic biomarkers**. Plasma/serum NfL is now well validated as a prognostic marker and axonal-injury readout (large multicenter cohorts, n≈2,900+; PMIDs 38609644, 38859579), and assay standardization is improving (PMID 38879065). But NfL is nonspecific (rises in many neurologic diseases) and measures damage, not the disease process. No surrogate biomarker accepted by regulators for accelerated approval outside special circumstances.

**4. Model validity failure.** The SOD1-G93A mouse produced hundreds of "positive" therapies and zero translational successes. Benatar's classic meta-analysis of mouse trials documented why: overexpression artifacts, small samples, non-blinded outcome assessment, publication bias, and treatment starting before pathology begins ("Lost in Translation") (PMID 17300945). Decades of pipeline were seeded from a model that represents ~2% of patients in an artificial state.

**5. Trial duration and endpoint arithmetic.** Median survival from onset is ~3–5 years (PMID 37296644); trials run 6–18 months against noisy, slow-declining scales (ALSFRS-R). Effect sizes of 0.4 points/month require hundreds of patients and years to detect — and can be false positives (CENTAUR vs PHOENIX). Survival endpoints need longer, larger, costlier trials than most sponsors will fund. Platform trials like HEALEY mitigate this structurally and have delivered rapid, clean negatives — arguably the field's most valuable recent methodological advance (PMIDs 40067821, 40067754).

**Bottom line:** The field has moved from empirical serendipity (riluzole) toward mechanism-targeted medicine (tofersen), and genetics-first strategies are finally working where targets are crisp. But every broad-spectrum attempt has failed because we intervene late, measure crudely, in a heterogeneous syndrome whose shared final pathways are druggable only in principle. The credible cure path runs through: presymptomatic detection in genetically at-risk people → early targeted knockdown/modification → NfL-guided monitoring → combination therapy for sporadic disease once TDP-43 or inflammatory nodes become tractable.
